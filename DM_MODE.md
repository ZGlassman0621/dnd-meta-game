# DM Mode — Design

DM Mode inverts the game. The user is the Dungeon Master. The AI plays a party of four distinct player characters who react to the user's narration, argue with each other, declare actions, and ask the DM to resolve them.

This is a different product than the rest of the app. Player Mode is "AI runs the world for me." DM Mode is "I run the world for an AI table." The two modes share infrastructure (sessions, chronicles, prompt caching, marker discipline) but the prompt, the surface, and the success criteria diverge.

This doc explains the design intent. For engineering detail (file paths, marker syntax, schema), see CLAUDE.md and the linked services.

---

## Why this mode exists

A new DM has nobody to run for. A solo DM with three free hours and no group can't get reps. The bar for "I want to try DMing" is currently *find four humans, schedule, prep enough material, commit*. DM Mode collapses that bar to *open the app, generate a party, narrate*. The AI provides four distinct voices, ongoing tensions, decisions to react to, and a campaign memory that builds across sessions.

The design target is **a real D&D table reaction surface** — characters who disagree, ask questions, push back, get hurt, argue about the loot, and have secrets they protect. Not four cooperative bots that all say "great idea, DM!"

---

## Design principles

**Four genuinely distinct people, not four colored skins.** The single most important constraint. The party generator enforces class diversity, alignment spread, and four genuinely different speaking styles. The system prompt opens with: *"If a reader covers the character names, they MUST be able to tell who is speaking from the dialogue alone."*

**The DM is god.** The user's word is law. Characters may argue with NPCs and each other, but the AI accepts every DM ruling and description without question. When the DM says it rains, it rains. When the DM says the door is locked, the door is locked. This is the inverse of Player Mode where the AI is the source of world truth.

**The AI declares intentions, the DM resolves.** The AI never rolls dice, never decides skill check outcomes, never narrates whether an attack hits, never describes the room a character walks into. It declares — *"Mira would like to check the door for traps (Investigation +5)"* — and stops. The DM rolls. The DM narrates the result. Then the AI reacts.

**Disagreement is mandatory.** At any meaningful decision point, at least two characters should have different opinions, rooted in their specific alignment, ideals, and flaws — not random contrarianism. A morally ambiguous Chaotic Neutral character and a Lawful Good paladin should not agree about how to handle the captured bandit.

**Silence is characterization.** Not all four speak every turn. 2–3 responses per DM prompt is normal. A brooding character should stay silent more often than a chatty one. *Silence is valid characterization* is in the prompt verbatim.

**No echoing.** Characters react to the *sentiment* of what the DM said, not by parroting the words back. When an NPC says something memorable, the character responds in their own voice — through action, expression, or original reflection. This is the second-most-important rule and it gets its own slot in both ABSOLUTE RULES and FINAL REMINDER.

**Secrets are protected.** Each character has a secret. The AI does not reveal it casually. Secrets surface only at dramatically perfect moments — under narrative pressure, when another character directly confronts the issue, or when the DM has earned the beat.

## The party generator

A new party is generated by Opus from a small config: setting, tone, level. The generator enforces:

| Constraint | Why |
|---|---|
| 4 different classes covering frontline / ranged / divine / arcane | Real party shape; gives the DM tactical variety in combat |
| 4 different alignments — at least one morally ambiguous, at least one with a strong moral compass | Disagreement comes from values, not personality quirks |
| 4 voice archetypes (formal/measured, terse/practical, chatty/humor-deflecting, thoughtful/awkward) | Voice diversity is the make-or-break quality of the mode |
| Minimum 3 specific inter-party tensions, with at least one secret-that-would-damage-trust | Drama is pre-loaded; the table doesn't need to manufacture it |
| Interlocking backstories (a specific reason these four are together) | "Met at a tavern" is forbidden; the party has shared origin gravity |

Each character ships with: race / class / subclass / background / level-appropriate stats / equipment / spells / personality block (traits, ideals, bonds, flaws, motivation, fear, secret, quirk, mannerism), voice description, speaking style, combat style, social style, moral tendencies, and a generated chat color (#60a5fa blue, #c084fc purple, #10b981 green, #f59e0b amber).

The party also gets a name (e.g. "The Reluctant Accord", "Ashfall Company") and a `party_concept` — the in-world reason these four travel together. Both are persisted.

## Inter-party dynamics — the evolution layer

The generated tensions are a starting state, not a permanent one. Sessions evolve them. After every session, Sonnet runs a relationship-extraction pass that updates a per-pair `party_relationships` block on each character with two scalars:

- **Warmth** — emotional closeness / affection
- **Trust** — reliability / faith in that person

These are tracked **directionally**: A's warmth toward B is independent of B's warmth toward A. Both numbers persist across sessions and feed into the next session's prompt. The prompt explicitly tells the AI to play the *current* relationship state — *a character with warmth: -3 toward someone is NOT friendly to them* — not the original tension.

Updates happen through the `[BOND_SHIFT]` marker, which the AI emits at the end of a response when a moment meaningfully changes how one character feels about another. Most messages have zero bond shifts. The marker carries: From, To, Warmth delta (-2..+2), Trust delta (-2..+2), one-sentence reason. Multiple markers per response are allowed for different pairs.

This is what keeps the party from feeling static. After 20 sessions, the alliance map is unrecognizable — old enemies have softened, old friendships have cracked, and the AI plays each pair from where they actually are.

## Markers

DM Mode uses a different marker set than Player Mode. The shared discipline (uppercase tags in brackets, server-side detection, regex-strippable for clean display) is the same; the surface area is different.

| Marker | What the AI emits | What the server does |
|---|---|---|
| `[SKILL_CHECK: Character="Name" Skill="Perception" Modifier="+5"]` | A character wants to attempt a check | Surfaces a pending roll in the DiceRoller's queue for the DM to resolve |
| `[ATTACK: Character="Name" Target="Goblin" Weapon="Longbow" AttackBonus="+7"]` | A character declares a combat attack | Surfaces a pending attack roll |
| `[CAST_SPELL: Character="Name" Spell="Healing Word" Target="Dorn" Level="1"]` | A character casts a leveled spell | Surfaces the cast (DM may want to roll the heal/save); spend the slot via the spell-slot endpoint |
| `[BOND_SHIFT: From=… To=… Warmth=… Trust=… Reason=…]` | A moment changed a per-pair relationship | Updates `party_relationships` on the From character, queues for chronicle |
| `[PARTY_ARGUMENT: …]` | An argument escalated between characters | Currently stripped from display; reserved for future argument-tracking |

All markers are stripped from narrative text before display via `cleanDMModeNarrative()`. The DiceRoller panel shows the pending rolls; the DM rolls them; the result goes back via `POST /:sessionId/roll-result` and the AI reacts in its next turn.

The DM also has free-roll capability (the DiceRoller has a quick-roll palette and a history) for things the AI didn't surface as a check — secret rolls, encounter setup, etc.

## The OOC channel

Prefix a message with `OOC:` and it becomes out-of-character. The UI renders it in purple. The AI is told it's the DM speaking *to* the players about their characters — character-creation feedback, scheduling notes, table talk, "hey, can your wizard not blow up the tavern this time." OOC messages don't trigger marker processing, don't count toward chronicle extraction, and don't shift bond scores. This is the safety valve for a real-table conversation that isn't roleplay.

## Memory across sessions

DM Mode has its own memory architecture parallel to Player Mode's chronicle system:

### `dm_mode_chronicles`
After each session, Sonnet runs an extraction pass that produces a structured chronicle: summary, key decisions (with who decided), NPCs involved (with role, location, race, age, personality, status, disposition, connections), locations visited, plot threads (with status), combat encounters, items gained, character moments (per character), mood, cliffhanger. Every chronicle row injects into the next session's system prompt — the party's history is canon and the AI is told to reference it naturally, not via exposition dumps.

### `dm_mode_npcs` (NPC Codex)
Auto-synced from chronicle extraction. Cross-session NPC tracking with voice-notes column, sessions-appeared array, first-seen / last-seen, manual edit support. The Codex panel is where the DM sees the campaign's NPC roster build up over time. The DM-Mode prompt builder uses an `aggregateNpcs` pass that merges chronicles with manual edits, fills-not-overwrites stable traits (race, class_profession, age, personality, connections), and always updates location / status / disposition to the latest. Recurring NPCs sort to the top.

### `dm_mode_plot_threads`
Auto-synced from chronicles with manual override support. A thread is "resolved" if its most recent chronicle mention says so. Threads carry tags (player-set), source (`auto` vs manual), first-seen / resolved-session. The aggregator splits into active and resolved lists; active threads inject into the prompt as ongoing storylines.

### `dm_mode_prep` (Campaign Prep)
A flat single-table prep system with six discriminated content types: `npc`, `enemy` (full 5e stat blocks), `location`, `lore`, `treasure`, `session_notes`. The DM uses this to write material *before* sessions; the in-session Prep panel lets them pull a stat block or a location note into the prompt. Prep is parked next to the campaign, not piped into every prompt automatically — it's a reference, not memory.

## In-session UI surfaces

The DM Mode session screen exposes:

- **Multi-character chat segments.** Each `**Name:**` block in the AI response is parsed by `parseCharacterSegments()` and rendered in the character's color, with their portrait/initial. This is the dominant visual register of the mode and what makes "four people are talking" land.
- **PartyView** (slide-in). Stats, spells, personality, current relationships per character. The DM uses this to remind themselves who has Bond X with Whom, what the secret is, what the AC is.
- **DiceRoller** (slide-in). Pending rolls queue from AI markers + free-roll palette + roll history. This is the DM's mechanical workbench — every check / attack / spell the AI declared is here, ready to resolve.
- **DMCoachingPanel** (slide-in). Sonnet-generated tips: a scene tip ("what's working, what could be better"), an encounter suggestion, a character hook (a specific opportunity to engage one of the four PCs based on their personality / secret / flaw). Plus a static DC reference table (Trivial 5 → Nearly Impossible 30) and beginner-DM tips. Sonnet is used here for cost — coaching is high-frequency and needs to feel cheap.
- **Reference panels.** Equipment, Spells (300+), Rules, Effect tracker (round countdown + concentration). Same panels available in Player Mode, scoped to the AI's party.

## Session lifecycle

```
1. Generate party (Opus)         →  4 characters with diverse class/alignment/voice + tensions
2. Start session (Opus)          →  First session opening, full system prompt with chronicles
3. Continuations (Sonnet)        →  Subsequent turns; cheaper, fast
4. End session                   →  Sonnet extracts chronicle + relationships + NPC codex + plot threads
5. Living state                  →  Game-day advances, party_relationships persisted, party_data updated
```

The player-mode pattern of "Opus for the opening, Sonnet for the rest" applies. The opening is the highest-leverage prose moment in a campaign — the prose density needs to be heavy, voices need to land hard, the world needs to feel present. From there, Sonnet carries the table at lower cost.

## What "good DM Mode" looks like

A successful DM Mode session should make the user feel like:

- **They're running a table.** They prepped (or didn't), they narrated the scene, the players reacted in ways that surprised them, they had to make a ruling, they rolled some dice, the players argued about a moral choice, someone took a risk and rolled badly, and a relationship shifted because of how the DM handled it.
- **The four characters are not interchangeable.** They could swap characters' lines in their head and be wrong. They have favorite party members. They notice when one is silent.
- **Choices have weight.** A bond_shift after an OOC argument actually changed how the warlock treats the cleric next session. A secret revealed on session 7 reshapes the party's dynamic for session 8 forward.
- **The campaign accumulates.** Session 4 references something that happened in session 1. The NPC in session 6 remembers the party from session 3. Plot threads from session 2 are still alive in session 9.

The failure modes the system is built against:

- **Four friendly bots.** Solved by mandatory disagreement, alignment spread, secrets, and the inter-party tension scaffold.
- **Echoing.** Solved by an ABSOLUTE RULE plus FINAL REMINDER repetition, plus example-based instruction (RIGHT vs WRONG examples in the prompt).
- **The AI rolls its own dice.** Solved by hard rules + example-based instruction + the marker pattern that surfaces a check as a *request*, not a result.
- **The AI narrates the world.** Solved by `PLAYER AGENCY — FORBIDDEN` rule explicitly listing NPCs / environment / weather / world events / new locations as off-limits, with the DM authority rule reinforcing it.
- **Static party.** Solved by the bond-shift evolution layer: warmth/trust scores update directionally per session and feed into the next prompt's relationship block.

## Cost & model split

- **Party generation**: Opus. One-time per party. High-stakes — bad generation taints the whole campaign.
- **First session opening**: Opus. Highest-leverage prose moment.
- **Continuation sessions**: Sonnet (with the Opus default toggle in Player Mode not currently extended to DM Mode — see PROJECT_TODO.md / FUTURE_FEATURES.md if cost analysis recommends an opt-up).
- **Coaching tips**: Sonnet. High-frequency, low-stakes, fast.
- **Chronicle extraction + relationship extraction + NPC codex sync**: Sonnet. Structured JSON, doesn't need prose density.

## Open / deferred design

- **Opus toggle for DM Mode continuations.** The Player Mode prose-quality finding (Opus is the differentiator at session-continuation prose density) hasn't been ported to DM Mode. Whether the four-voice constraint amplifies or dampens the Opus advantage is an open question — needs a playtest with an Opus toggle.
- **`[PARTY_ARGUMENT]` marker.** Reserved in the cleanup regex but not yet processed. The intent is to track escalating arguments as a structured event for chronicle / relationship extraction, distinct from individual bond shifts.
- **Cross-party memory.** A user with multiple parties has parallel campaign worlds. There is no mechanism today to let an NPC from one party show up in another, or to import a chronicled location across parties.
- **Session-end relationship summary UI.** The bond shifts apply silently. A post-session "here's what changed between characters this session" view would make the evolution layer visible — currently the DM has to compare the PartyView relationship block before/after to see what moved.
- **Live multi-DM tables.** Out of scope for the current product but implied as a future direction — multiple humans DMing simultaneously, AI as the players for *both* tables, with a shared world. Not on roadmap.
