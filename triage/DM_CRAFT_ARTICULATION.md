### What Good DMing Looks Like in my Forever D&D Game

In a game intended to last me essentially forever, I think there are several cornerstones to how I would define what good DMing looks like: the ability to tell a cohesive story; the ability to interact with both the player (me) and my characters over different campaigns in a meaningful way; makes decisions that increase the fun without risking the story; and builds a world that is both believable and fantastic. Let me break these down:

## The Ability to Tell a Cohesive Story
The DM is a storyteller. Ultimately, the DM is THE storyteller: the source of the story being told through the gameplay. An arc may be generated at the initial start to the campaign, but the DM is the narrator making things happen. The way I understand D&D is that the core gameplay loop is: the DM describes a situation, the player describes what they want to do in that situation, and dice are rolled to determine if what the player wants to do gets done. This is an oversimplification, but this loop is in everything that happens in D&D. And so, when it comes to storytelling, the DM’s role really is to allow this gameplay loop to happen hidden beneath the story that it creates. Further, the ability to tell a cohesive story is the ability to plan story arcs that may not come to fruition for months or even years of gameplay. Remember, this is meant to be my forever game - having story threads that present themselves in small ways now and then but don’t fully come about for months to come is, to me, a sign that the world is real and believable. For example: Rivelious Freeborn (Riv to his friends) is a Cleric of Lathander. Riv comes to the small village of Embervale and befriends a cobbler of good reputation. That cobbler has a daughter, mentioned in passing, as a scholar at Candlekeep. Quests happen, time passes, and Riv is called away. When next he makes his way through Embervale, he meets the young woman at her father’s shop. He asks after her studies and she regales him with a story of two young men who recently arrived in Candlekeep. The story is interrupted by the girl’s father, who remarks on how nice she and Riv look standing near one another and the girl, clearly embarrassed by the implications, scurries off. Another few in-game years go by and Riv comes to Embervale yet again, this time just in time to find the town in mourning. Two young men who previously studied at Candlekeep followed their rival to her hometown and murdered her and her father. Potentially months of real-time story; threads that wend their way into the overarching story and which may present opportunities for future storylines or may be entirely disconnected to the main quest. That, to me, is something a great DM can do.

## The Ability to Interact with Both the Player (Me) and My Characters Over Different Campaigns in a Meaningful Way

The DM represents every interaction my character has within the story. Since I don’t play multiplayer the way D&D is traditionally, the DM has to assume every other role. The differentiation of how the DM does that makes or breaks the game world. The DM should recognize the differences in the world state between characters, allowing the world to feel like its own unique slice of Faerun (unless otherwise agreed upon as a shared world between my characters - which isn’t something we’ve discussed yet, but which could make a cool Future Feature!)

## The Ability to Make Decisions that Increase the Fun without Risking the Story

DMs are the referees of D&D. They recognize that die rolls control most things and it’s up to them to make the decisions that increase or decrease the pacing, the fun, the action, and more. A great DM will be able to recognize based on player input just how the player is responding to the world they’ve created; and they should adjust the outcome of rolls to match. There’s a limit there, of course - a Nat 1 should always be an abysmal result but it can also be an incredibly lucky way to find something (Riv attempts to solve a puzzle by pressing a pressure switch. I roll a Natural 1, a Critical Failure and, through a series of ridiculous happenstance, a pillar collapses, injuring Riv and breaking a hole in the wall Riv was meant to get through by solving the puzzle). By the same token, a Nat 20 should always be an incredible success, but against a 30 DC can still fail in a way that feels like a win (Riv attempts to calm the Dragon King. I roll a Critical Success but still fall short of the 30 DC required to calm the Dragon King. Riv succeeds in getting the Dragon King to reveal where he keeps his most prized treasures, but the fight still proceeds). I saw online this line that I really like: “it is critical that RPGs have someone play the role of referee, so that they can use their [human] judgement in nuanced situations”. Maybe the rules say one thing, but gameplay really needs things to work out anyway. Maybe a character is out of spell slots but for something really awesome to happen, the DM allows that character to pray to their god for intervention. From that same website: “Keeping rules loose is important because there are situations where bending a rule, or creating one on the fly, will prompt a more interesting story.”

## Finally, a Good DM Builds a World that is both Believable and Fantastic

To me, Faerun is an exciting location just because of how much creativity there is to create. A player can journey through cities powered by magic to others powered by clockwork devices and back to villages in medieval eras grounded fully in survival and realism. A good DM builds these systems and allows them to interconnect. I don’t know really how much better to explain this concept, but that’s just something I feel about DMing in this game - it requires the ability to build a world that consistently feels real, lived in, but at the same time fantastic and mythological - where gods and monsters roam just as much as packs of wild animals and just as sure as the changing of the seasons. Where farmers can predict the weather based on natural patterns and alchemists can brew potions to restore life to the dead. Where forgotten histories exist, dead languages are found carved on walls, dragons fly, giants fight, and werewolves prowl, all while kingdoms war over trade agreements and land rights.
